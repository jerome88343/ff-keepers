The Keeper Tax

Chad put a Player Tax proposal up and it cleared the bar to be voted on. Ryan, Phil and Jeremy all said the same thing back: make it hit sooner and harder. Here are two versions built from that feedback, and what other leagues have learned solving the same problem.
What is actually on the table

The Player Tax is an add-on. Everything we already do stays exactly as it is — first keeper year costs the round you drafted him, every year after that costs his current expert consensus rank, and the luxury tax still applies to bumps. The Player Tax is a separate cash fee stacked on top for holding the same guy year after year.

So the question is not whether keepers get more expensive. They already do. The question is whether the extra fee is big enough to change anybody's mind.

Why Phil's objection lands. Ja'Marr Chase costs a 1st and will cost a 1st every year he stays great. Somebody will pay that happily. Adding ten dollars in year four, climbing to thirty by year eight, does not make anyone blink. That is Phil's point: you still never see Chase, Gibbs or Bijan in a draft again. The fee has to be large enough to be a real decision, or it is just a small donation to the pot.

What other leagues do

Keeper leagues have two levers for this problem. Version A pulls one, Version B pulls the other.
Escalating cost works

Making a keeper more expensive every year you hold him is the standard fix, and it is what we already do through ECR. The research is clear that escalation alone is not always enough if the escalation is cheap relative to the player.

The other main lever

A hard time limit — two or three years and the player goes back in the pool no matter what. Described as easy to administer and the cleanest way to stop any one player being locked to a roster indefinitely.

The middle path

The most widely used setup pairs a cap with escalating cost. Both levers together. Leagues that use only one end up as a permanent bargain hunt where elite players never re-enter the draft at fair value — the exact failure Phil named.

The two versions

Both are add-ons. Neither touches how keeper cost works today — drafted round the first year, ECR after that, luxury tax on bumps. Both keep 4 keepers, max 3 WR, max 2 discounts, traded players carrying their status, and only free agency resetting the clock.

Version B

The Hard Cap

No new money. A player can only be kept a set number of straight years, then he goes back in the draft no matter what. Cost while you hold him works exactly like it does today.

Why you would want it
  • Answers Phil directly. You will see Chase and CMC again, on a date everyone knows.
  • Nobody can buy their way out of it.
  • One extra rule to remember, not a new pricing system.
  • Keeps a real draft interesting every single year.
What you are accepting
  • You lose a guy you found and developed, on a clock.
  • Almost no new money into the pot.
  • Year four gets weird — do you pay $50 for a last ride or let him go early.

What actually separates them

One question, and it is not really about money.

Version A says: hold him as long as you like, but it costs real money and it climbs fast. The league gets paid instead of the player coming back.

Version B says: money should not decide it. There is a hard limit, everyone hits it, and every star goes back in the pool on schedule.

Both are defensible. They are just different leagues. Vote for the one you would rather play in.

Still open either way: what counts as a consecutive year when a player is traded mid-season, and whether the Player Tax stacks on top of the existing luxury tax for a bumped keeper. Chad's draft says traded players carry their status and only free agency resets the clock — that part is clean and both versions keep it.

Threshold: a rule change needs 8 of 12 yes votes. This one already has enough interest to go to a vote, so what is left is agreeing on which version is on the ballot.
